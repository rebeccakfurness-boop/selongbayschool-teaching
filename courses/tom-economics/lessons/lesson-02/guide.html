Lesson 2 — Teaching Guide & Script

Syllabus ref: 2.4.3 Market disequilibrium & 2.5 Price changes
Linked materials: worksheet.md · slides.html · flashcards.txt — all four cover this same syllabus point.

Tom already knows demand, supply and market equilibrium solidly (confirmed via the Term 1 recap worksheet) — don't re-teach equilibrium from scratch, just touch it briefly as the anchor before introducing what's new: what happens when price isn't at equilibrium.

Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson Tom can:
1. Define market disequilibrium and distinguish a shortage (quantity demanded > quantity supplied) from a surplus (quantity supplied > quantity demanded).
2. Read shortages/surpluses off a demand–supply diagram and a schedule.
3. Explain that price changes are caused by shifts in demand and/or supply, and state their consequence for quantity sold.

The running example: padel court bookings

Tom's local padel club has a fixed number of courts. The interesting thing: the same fixed supply of courts faces wildly different demand at different times of day — perfect for disequilibrium.

Timing breakdown (30 minutes)

0–3 min — Quick anchor (recap, don't re-teach)
"Remind me — at equilibrium, what's true about quantity demanded and quantity supplied?" (They're equal.) Move on fast — this is confirmed knowledge, not new content.

3–12 min — Disequilibrium: shortage vs surplus
Set up the club's evening peak slot (6–8pm weekdays): tons of people want to play after school, but only a handful of courts exist. At the club's normal flat price, quantity demanded > quantity supplied at that price → this is a shortage.
Now the weekday 9am slot: almost nobody wants to play, but the same courts are sitting empty. At the normal price, quantity supplied > quantity demanded → this is a surplus.
Draw both on a D/S diagram: shortage = price line below the equilibrium price; surplus = price line above it. Get Tom to sketch both.

12–20 min — How the market self-corrects, and 2.5 causes of price changes
Ask: "If you ran the club, what would you actually do about the 6pm shortage? What about the empty 9am courts?" Lead him to: raise the evening price (reduces quantity demanded, moves toward the true — higher — equilibrium for that time slot) and cut the morning price (attracts more bookings, moves toward the true — lower — equilibrium for that slot). This is the price mechanism clearing disequilibrium.
Reinforce with a second example Tom already half-knows: a discontinued Lego set. Once a set is retired, supply is fixed/shrinking while demand often keeps going (or grows, from collectors) → shortage at the old retail price → resale prices rise on the secondary market until quantity demanded falls back to match the fixed quantity available. Same mechanism, different market.

20–26 min — 2.5 Price changes: causes and consequences
State plainly: price changes are caused by shifts in demand and/or supply (not by "the market deciding" — some underlying determinant moved). The consequence is a change in the quantity bought and sold. Don't get pulled into exact magnitudes yet — flag explicitly: "How much quantity changes when price changes is a whole separate idea — price elasticity of demand — which is the next two lessons." This sets up PED cleanly without teaching it early.

26–30 min — Guided practice + wrap-up
Run worksheet Section B verbally. Set self-directed worksheet + flashcards for later. Preview next lesson: "Next time: why a price rise crushes sales of some things but barely dents others — price elasticity of demand."

Common misconceptions to watch for

  1. Movement along vs shift of a curve. A price change alone causes a movement along the existing curve (this lesson). A shift of the whole curve is caused by a non-price determinant changing (already covered in demand/supply lessons) — don't let the two blur together.
  2. "Shortage" doesn't mean zero stock exists. It specifically means quantity demanded exceeds quantity supplied at the current price — the courts still physically exist, they're just fully booked.
  3. Surplus is not "nobody wants it at all." It means supply exceeds demand at that price — a lower price could still clear it.
  4. Assuming prices adjust instantly and perfectly — real markets (like the padel club) take a manager's decision to actually change the price; it's not automatic.
