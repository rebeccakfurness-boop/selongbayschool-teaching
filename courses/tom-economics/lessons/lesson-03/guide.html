Lesson 3 — Teaching Guide & Script

Syllabus ref: 2.6 Price elasticity of demand (PED) (2.6.1 Definition · 2.6.2 Calculation & interpretation · 2.6.3 Determinants)
Linked materials: worksheet.html / worksheet.docx · slides.html / slides.pptx · flashcards.txt — all cover this same syllabus point.

Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson Tom can:

  1. Define price elasticity of demand (PED).
  2. Calculate PED using the formula and classify the result (perfectly inelastic, inelastic, unitary, elastic, perfectly elastic).
  3. Explain the main determinants of whether demand for a good is elastic or inelastic.

Timing breakdown (30 minutes)

0–4 min — Hook
Ask Tom: "If Tom's Print Co. raised the price of custom padel grips by 10%, would he lose a few customers, or a lot? What would that depend on?" Let him speculate — don't correct yet, just surface the intuition that responsiveness varies by product.

4–8 min — Definition and formula
State it plainly: PED measures how responsive quantity demanded is to a change in price.

PED = (% change in quantity demanded) ÷ (% change in price)

Flag immediately: PED is technically always negative (price up → quantity down, or vice versa — the law of demand), but economists usually just discuss its size, ignoring the minus sign.

8–16 min — Worked calculations
Walk through both worked examples on the worksheet together:
- Padel club casual court bookings: price $20→$22 (+10%), quantity 100→80 bookings/week (−20%). PED = −20/10 = −2 → elastic (plenty of substitute sports/venues).
- A retired Lego set on the resale market: price $50→$55 (+10%), quantity 40→38 sets/month (−5%). PED = −5/10 = −0.5 → inelastic (collectors have no substitute for that exact set).

For each, get Tom to say out loud which category it falls into: perfectly inelastic (PED = 0) · inelastic (0 to 1) · unitary (exactly 1) · elastic (greater than 1) · perfectly elastic (infinite). Sketch the demand curve shape for each category — steep/vertical for inelastic, flat/horizontal for elastic.

16–24 min — Determinants of PED
Go through each, always landing on a Tom's Print Co./padel/Lego example:

  • Substitutes — more/closer substitutes = more elastic. A generic Lego set (many toy alternatives) vs. a specific retired set (no substitute).
  • Necessity vs luxury — necessities are more inelastic. A padel club's court resurfacing (has to happen) vs. Tom's novelty keychains (impulse buy).
  • Proportion of income spent — bigger share of income = more elastic. A cheap grip (tiny share of pocket money, barely noticed) vs. a padel club's annual membership (big share, very noticeable).
  • Addictiveness/habit — habit-forming goods are more inelastic (classic textbook example: cigarettes).
  • Time period — demand is more elastic in the long run as people find alternatives. Tom's regular customers might keep buying grips short-term out of habit even at a higher price, but switch to a rival supplier over a few months.
  • Width of definition of the good — broadly defined goods are more inelastic than narrowly defined ones. "3D-printed products" in general (hard to avoid) vs. "Tom's specific grip design" narrowly (easy to switch to a competitor's).

24–28 min — Guided practice
Run through worksheet Section C verbally before Tom writes anything.

28–30 min — Wrap-up
Set the worksheet + flashcards for self-directed time. Preview next lesson: "Next time — what PED means for how much money Tom actually makes when he changes his prices."

Common misconceptions to watch for

  1. Sign confusion. PED is mathematically negative, but "elastic vs inelastic" is about the size of the number, not its sign. Don't let a negative PED get mistaken for "demand fell."
  2. Elastic ≠ "big price change." Elastic means quantity demanded is highly responsive relative to the price change — a small price change causing a big swing in quantity, not just "a big number somewhere."
  3. Confusing PED determinants with demand shifters. These explain how responsive demand is, not what shifts the demand curve — don't blend this with the shift-vs-movement distinction from Lesson 2.
  4. Assuming steepness alone tells you everything. A steep-looking curve is a useful visual cue for inelastic at this level, but remind Tom the real test is always the calculated percentage change, not just how the line looks.
