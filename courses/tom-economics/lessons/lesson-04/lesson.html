PED: Revenue & Significance

If Tom raises his prices, does he end up richer — or poorer? First, two quick fixes from last time.

Recap

Quick PED Refresher

Last lesson you learned to calculate and classify PED. Before we build on it today, let's remind ourselves of the formula.

PED = %ΔQd ÷ %ΔP
Work out the % change in price, work out the % change in quantity demanded, then divide quantity's % change by price's % change. Size < 1 = inelastic. Size > 1 = elastic. Size = 1 = unitary.

Hook

Today's Real Question

Tom puts up the price of his padel grips. More money per grip sold... but does he end up with more total money? Guess — and tell me why.
New idea

Total Revenue

Revenue isn't profit — it's simply the total money coming in from sales, before any costs are subtracted.

TR = P × Q
If Tom sells 60 grips at $8 each, his revenue is $480 — that's all the money changing hands, regardless of what it cost him to make them.
Why isn't this obvious?

Two Things Move at Once

When Tom changes his price, it doesn't just change P. Because of PED, it also changes Q — quantity sold moves too. And TR depends on both.

Raise the price, and P goes up — that alone would raise revenue. But quantity demanded falls at the same time — that alone would lower revenue. Which effect wins depends entirely on PED. That's the whole lesson, and it's why "just raise the price" isn't always the right call.

So What's the Actual Rule?

Look back at those three results. Notice the pattern isn't about whether price went up — it's about how elastic the demand was.

When demand is inelastic, quantity barely reacts, so the higher price mostly just sticks — revenue rises. When demand is elastic, quantity reacts more than price did, so the loss in sales outweighs the higher price — revenue falls. The same logic works in reverse for a price cut.
