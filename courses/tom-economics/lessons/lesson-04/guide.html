Lesson 4 — Comprehensive Teaching Script

Syllabus ref: 2.6 Price elasticity of demand — continued (2.6.4 PED, consumer expenditure & firms' revenue · 2.6.5 Significance of PED)
Linked materials: worksheet.html / worksheet.docx · slides.html / slides.pptx · flashcards.txt

This is a full script, not just a lesson outline — it's written so you can follow it slide-by-slide live, with what to say, what to ask, what she might ask back, and extra depth if she wants it. You don't need to read it word for word — paraphrase in your own voice — but every idea it covers should land before you move on.

Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson she can:

  1. Calculate total revenue (TR = P × Q) before and after a price change.
  2. Explain why revenue rises or falls after a price change, depending on whether demand is elastic or inelastic.
  3. Explain why PED matters for the pricing decisions of firms, for consumers' budgets, and for government tax policy.

Before you start: what this lesson is responding to

Her Lesson 2 worksheet showed two things worth fixing early, and one real gap worth re-teaching. Slides 2–3 handle this directly — don't skip them even though they're not "new content," they're load-bearing for everything that follows today.

Slide-by-slide script

Slide 1 — Title

Say: "Today we're answering a question that actually decides whether Tom's Print Co. makes more or less money: if he raises his prices, does he end up richer or poorer?"

Slide 2 — Quick fix: land vs. free goods

Say: "Quick one before we start — in your factors of production notes, you had land down as a free good. Let's untangle that, because they're actually two separate ideas from two different lessons."
Ask her: "What's the actual difference between land as a factor of production, and a free good?"
What a good answer sounds like: Land (1.2) is a factor of production — a resource used to produce things, like the raw filament material. A free good (1.1.3) is something with no opportunity cost to obtain — like sunlight. Land is usually scarce and often costs money (rent); most free goods aren't factors of production at all.
If she's stuck: Point back to Lesson 1's Tom's Print Co. example — the filament (land) has a real cost and a real supplier. Sunlight doesn't cost Tom anything and nobody's selling it to him.
If she asks "so is land ever free?": Occasionally something classed as land-type (like a naturally occurring resource nobody's claimed) could be free, but for exam purposes, don't conflate the two categories — they're tested separately.

Slide 3 — Quick fix: causes vs. consequences of a price change

Say: "Second quick one. In Lesson 2 you described a shortage or surplus as what causes a price change. Really close, but the order's flipped — let's fix that now because today's whole lesson builds on it."
Ask her: "What actually causes a price to change in a market?"
What a good answer sounds like: A shift in demand and/or supply — caused by a change in one of their non-price determinants (income, tastes, cost of production, number of buyers/sellers, etc.). A shortage or surplus is what you get when price isn't at the new equilibrium yet — it's a symptom, not the cause.
If she's stuck: Walk it in order: something changes (e.g. more people want padel) → that shifts the demand curve → that creates a shortage at the old price → that is what pushes price up. The shortage is step 3, not step 1.
Extra context: This is exactly the distinction AO2 marks reward — "identify → explain the mechanism → state the effect" as a chain, not just naming the end state.

Slide 4 — Hook

Say: "So here's today's real question. Tom puts up the price of his padel grips. More money per grip sold... but does he actually end up with more total money? Guess, and tell me why."
Let her guess before moving on — don't confirm or deny yet, just note what she says so you can circle back to it after Slide 7.

Slide 5 — Total revenue formula

Say: "Revenue is just price times quantity — TR = P × Q. Simple formula, but it's the whole engine of today's lesson: a price change moves P, but it also moves Q, because of PED — and those two moves can fight each other."
Ask her: "If price goes up but quantity goes down, why isn't it obvious whether total revenue goes up or down?"
What a good answer sounds like: Because it depends on which effect is bigger — how much price rose vs. how much quantity fell. That's exactly what PED tells you.

Slide 6 — Worked example 1 setup: padel bookings (elastic)

Say: "Remember this one from last lesson — the padel club's 6–8pm slot. Price went from $20 to $22, up 10%. Quantity fell from 100 to 80 bookings a week, down 20%. We calculated PED = −2, elastic. Before I show you — guess: does revenue go up or down?"
Let her guess and give a reason before revealing.

Slide 7 — Reveal: revenue falls

Say: "TR before: $20 × 100 = $2000. TR after: $22 × 80 = $1760. Revenue actually fell, even though price went up! Because demand is elastic, quantity fell proportionally more than price rose, and that effect wins."
Ask her: "So if you ran the club, would raising the evening price be a good idea?"
What a good answer sounds like: It solves the shortage problem (fewer people wanting a slot), but it comes at a real revenue cost — a genuine trade-off, not a free win. Reward her if she spots that these are two different goals (fixing a shortage vs. maximising revenue) that don't automatically point the same way.
If she asks "so should the club never raise prices?": No — it depends what the club's actual goal is. If the goal is to ration the limited courts fairly, the price rise still makes sense even if revenue dips slightly.

Slide 8 — Worked example 2 setup: Lego resale (inelastic)

Say: "Now the retired Lego set. Price $50 to $55, up 10%. Quantity 40 to 38 sets, down 5%. PED was −0.5, inelastic. Guess again — revenue up or down this time?"

Slide 9 — Reveal: revenue rises

Say: "TR before: $50 × 40 = $2000. TR after: $55 × 38 = $2090. This time revenue rose. Because demand is inelastic, quantity barely moved, so the higher price mostly just... stuck."
Ask her: "What's the actual rule hiding in these two examples?"
What a good answer sounds like: When demand is elastic, a price rise loses more in quantity than it gains in price, so revenue falls. When demand is inelastic, a price rise loses very little quantity, so revenue rises. Don't give her the rule yet if she's close — let Slide 12 confirm it.

Slide 10 — Your turn: Tom's grips

Say: "Your turn to do the maths. Tom's grips: price $8 to $10, quantity 60 to 54 — same numbers as last lesson, where you found PED = −0.4, inelastic. Work out TR before and after."
Let her actually calculate this herself — resist doing it for her. Prompt with "what's the formula again?" if she stalls.

Slide 11 — Check: Tom's grips answer

Say: "Let's check — TR before: $8 × 60 = $480. TR after: $10 × 54 = $540. Revenue rose, and that tracks — inelastic demand again."
If she got it wrong: Find out whether it was the arithmetic or the concept. If she predicted revenue would fall despite inelastic demand, that's a sign the rule hasn't landed yet — go back to Slide 9's contrast before moving on.

Slide 12 — The rule (summary table)

Say: "Here's the whole lesson in one table. Inelastic demand: price up means revenue up, price down means revenue down — they move together. Elastic demand: price up means revenue down, price down means revenue up — they move opposite. Unitary: revenue doesn't change either way."
Ask her: "Without a calculator — if a firm knows its product has very elastic demand, should it raise or lower price to boost revenue?"
What a good answer sounds like: Lower it — elastic demand means a price cut brings in proportionally more extra customers than the discount costs, so revenue rises.

Slide 13 — Why it matters: firms, consumers, government

Say: "This isn't just a maths trick — PED actually drives real decisions. Firms use it to set prices for maximum revenue. Consumers feel it hardest when the things they can't avoid buying — inelastic necessities — go up in price, since they can't easily cut back. And governments use it to choose what to tax: taxing something inelastic, like fuel or cigarettes, raises a lot of reliable revenue because people keep buying it anyway."
Ask her: "Why might a government prefer to put a tax on cigarettes rather than on, say, a luxury holiday package?"
What a good answer sounds like: Cigarette demand is inelastic (habit-forming, few substitutes) so the tax raises substantial, predictable revenue without quantity falling much. A luxury holiday has elastic demand — a tax would cause a big drop in bookings, undermining the revenue the tax was meant to raise.
Extra context if she's interested: This is also why "sin taxes" are popular with governments even when the stated goal is to discourage consumption — inelastic demand means the tax doesn't actually reduce consumption much, but it does raise a lot of money. Worth flagging as a genuine policy tension.

Slide 14 — Quick check

Ask both questions verbally, out loud, no need to reveal anything on-screen — by now she's seen the pattern three times.
1. "A firm's product has PED = −3. It's thinking about a price rise. Good idea or bad idea, and why?" (Bad — highly elastic, revenue would fall.)
2. "A firm's product has PED = −0.2. Same question." (Good — very inelastic, revenue would rise.)

Slide 15 — Recap

Say: "TR = P × Q. Inelastic demand: price and revenue move together. Elastic demand: price and revenue move opposite. That's why PED matters to firms setting prices, consumers feeling the pinch, and governments choosing what to tax."

Slide 16 — Next lesson

Say: "Next time we do the supply-side version of all this — price elasticity of supply. Same structure, new letter."

Wrap-up

Set the worksheet for self-directed time, and mention flashcards.txt for NotebookLM. Circle back to her Slide 4 guess once you've done Slide 7 — if she originally guessed "more revenue," that's a good, honest teaching moment to name out loud: the intuitive answer and the correct answer aren't always the same, and that's exactly what PED is for.
