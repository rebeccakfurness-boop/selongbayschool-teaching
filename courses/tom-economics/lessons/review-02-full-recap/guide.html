A note before you start

This is a breadth pass, not a depth pass — the goal is to surface anything that's gone rusty before Lesson 9 starts a new sub-topic. Move briskly through each topic's recap cards; only slow down and re-explain from scratch if he genuinely can't answer, rather than as a matter of course. Budget roughly 2–3 minutes per topic. If one topic clearly needs more than a quick refresher, it's worth flagging that as a candidate for a dedicated re-teach slot later, rather than eating into this session's time.

Slide-by-slide script

Slide 1 — Title / roadmap

Say: "Today's a rapid tour back through everything since we started — quick recap, quick practice, nothing new. Anything that feels shaky is exactly what today is for."

Slide 2 — Factors of production

Run through all four cards. Ask him: "Which factor earns interest as its reward?" (Capital.) If land/labour/capital/enterprise come back instantly, move on — no need to re-derive Tom's Print Co examples unless he asks.

Slide 3 — Disequilibrium & price changes

Run through all four cards. Ask him: "What's the difference between a movement along a curve and a shift of a curve?" (A price change alone moves you along the curve; a non-price determinant changing shifts the whole curve.) This distinction is the one that blurs together most often — worth double-checking.

Slide 4 — PED: core ideas

Run through all four flip cards. Ask him: "What's the PED formula, in words?" (% change in quantity demanded ÷ % change in price.)

Slide 5 — PED: quick calculation

"Padel club coaching: price $20 to $25, that's +25%. Weekly attendance falls from 100 to 70, that's −30%. Calculate the PED and classify it." (PED = −30% ÷ 25% = −1.2 → elastic.)

Slide 6 — PED & total revenue

Using the same numbers: "Calculate total revenue before and after, and say whether it rose or fell." (TR before = $20 × 100 = $2,000. TR after = $25 × 70 = $1,750. Revenue fell by $250 — with elastic demand, a price rise always cuts revenue.)
Ask him: "Would the opposite be true if demand were inelastic instead?" (Yes — with inelastic demand, a price rise would raise revenue, since quantity falls proportionally less than price rises.)

Slide 7 — PES

Run through both flip cards, then: "Tom's Print Co custom nameplates: price $10 to $12, that's +20%. Weekly quantity supplied rises from 30 to 33, that's +10%. Calculate the PES and classify it." (PES = 10% ÷ 20% = 0.5 → inelastic.)

Slide 8 — Market economic system

Run through all four flip cards. Ask him: "What are the three questions a market economy answers, and how?" (What — whatever earns profit. How — however keeps costs lowest. For whom — whoever can pay.)

Slide 9 — Market failure: cost & externalities

Run through all four flip cards. Ask him: "What's the difference between private cost and social cost?" (Social cost = private cost + external cost — the full cost to society, not just the producer.)

Slide 10 — Market failure: goods

Run through all four flip cards. Ask him: "Why does the free-rider problem mean the market won't supply a public good at all?" (A private firm can't charge non-payers, so there's no profit in providing it — even though people want it.)

Slide 11 — Market failure: consequences

Run through all four flip cards. Ask him: "Why does a negative externality cause overproduction?" (The market only 'sees' private cost, so production continues past the point that's actually efficient for society.)

Slide 12 — Mixed economy: tax & subsidy

Run through all three flip cards. Ask him: "What happens to supply when government adds an indirect tax?" (It shifts left — price rises, quantity falls.)

Slide 13 — Mixed economy: price controls

Run through all four flip cards. Ask him: "Why does a maximum price below equilibrium cause a shortage?" (Quantity demanded exceeds quantity supplied at that artificially low price.)

Slide 14 — Which lesson is this from?

Read each of the seven scenarios and ask him to name which lesson/topic it belongs to, then discuss. This is the best single check of whether the ideas are staying distinct in his head rather than blurring together.

Slide 15 — Mixed quiz

Run the seven-question quiz, one from each major topic. Treat any miss here as the clearest signal of where a top-up is actually needed — more useful than the flip-card recap, since he can't just recognise the answer, he has to produce it.

Slide 16 — Recap + what's next

Say: "That's everything since we started — factors of production, disequilibrium, PED, PES, the market system, market failure, and government's response through tax, subsidy, and price controls. Next lesson finishes off Topic 2: the rest of government's toolkit — regulation, privatisation, nationalisation, and quotas."

Wrap-up

Set the worksheet for self-directed time — it's short and cross-topic by design, one or two questions per lesson rather than a deep dive into any single one. Note down anywhere he hesitated today; if a pattern shows up (e.g. PES determinants, or the externality-to-consequence link), that's worth a few minutes at the start of Lesson 9 before moving into new content.
