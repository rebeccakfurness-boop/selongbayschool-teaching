Market failure: When the price mechanism fails to allocate resources efficiently, leading to over- or under-production of a good or service compared to what's best for society.

Private cost: What the producer or consumer themselves actually pays. For Tom, the plastic and electricity behind an $8 padel grip.

External cost: A cost dumped on someone not involved in the transaction, e.g. the pollution cost paid by people living near the stream where Tom's plastic waste ends up.

Social cost: Private cost + external cost — the full, true cost to society of an economic activity. Whenever social cost is greater than private cost, that gap is where market failure comes from.

Private benefit and external benefit: The same private/external split applies to benefit as to cost — private benefit goes to the producer/consumer, external benefit spills onto a third party.

Externality: A cost or benefit of a transaction that spills over onto a third party not involved in it.

Negative production externality: A cost created while a good is being produced, imposed on someone uninvolved, e.g. Tom's plastic waste polluting the stream.

Negative consumption externality: A cost created while a good is being consumed, imposed on someone uninvolved, e.g. loud music from a late padel match disturbing neighbours.

Positive production externality: A benefit created as a side effect of production, received by someone who paid nothing for it, e.g. a beekeeper's bees pollinating a neighbouring farmer's crops.

Positive consumption externality: A benefit created as a side effect of consumption, received by someone who paid nothing for it, e.g. a flu vaccine lowering the chance of others catching the disease.

Private good: Excludable (non-payers can be stopped from using it) and rivalrous (one person's use reduces how much is left for others), e.g. a padel court booking.

Public good: Non-excludable (can't stop non-payers benefiting) and non-rivalrous (one person's use doesn't reduce another's), e.g. street lighting.

The free-rider problem: Because a public good is non-excludable, anyone can benefit without paying. No private firm can profit from providing it, so the market fails to supply it at all, even though people genuinely want it.

Merit good: Under-provided by the market because people undervalue its benefit to themselves, or ignore the positive externality it creates for others, e.g. education, healthcare, vaccination.

Demerit good: Over-provided by the market because people undervalue its cost to themselves, or ignore the negative externality it creates for others, e.g. cigarettes, junk food.

Socially efficient output: The amount of a good or service that's actually best for society as a whole, once all costs and benefits (not just private ones) are counted.

Market output: How much actually gets produced, based only on private cost and private benefit — differs from socially efficient output whenever an externality exists.

Overproduction: Producing more than the socially efficient amount, typically caused by negative externalities. The market only "sees" private cost, so production continues past the point it should stop.

Underproduction: Producing less than the socially efficient amount, typically caused by positive externalities or merit goods. The market only "sees" private benefit, ignoring the wider benefit to society.

Negative externalities and overproduction: Since social cost is higher than private cost, and the market ignores the difference, more gets produced than is actually best for society, e.g. Tom's Print Co continuing to pollute the stream at full output.

Positive externalities/merit goods and underproduction: Since social benefit is higher than private benefit, and the market ignores the difference, less gets provided than would actually be best for society, e.g. a free coding workshop offered less often than ideal.

Public goods and zero provision: Because of the free-rider problem, a private firm can't capture profit from providing a public good, so the market supplies none at all, even though people clearly want it.

Consequence for consumers: May pay for goods that quietly harm others (negative externalities), or miss out on merit goods that would genuinely benefit them.

Consequence for producers: No market signal to change, since nobody is actually charging them for the externality they create — from their own perspective, nothing looks wrong.

Consequence for government: Must judge whether the gap between market output and socially efficient output is large enough to justify a policy response, and how.

Government's toolkit (preview): Taxes (discourage demerit goods/negative externalities), subsidies (encourage merit goods/positive externalities), regulation (rules and limits), and direct provision (government supplies public goods itself) — covered in full next lesson.
