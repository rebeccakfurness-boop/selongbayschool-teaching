Hook

Two Things We Left Hanging

Remember the plastic waste from Tom's printer ending up in the stream? And the streetlights nobody privately installs on the path to the padel club?

Both are examples of the market getting something wrong. Today we put proper economic names on exactly what's going wrong in each case — and a few more ways it can happen too.
New idea

What Is Market Failure?

Market failure happens when the price mechanism fails to allocate resources efficiently — leading to too much or too little of something being produced, compared to what's actually best for society.

In the stream-pollution example: the market price of a padel grip only reflects what it costs Tom to make it. It says nothing about the cost of the pollution to people who live near the stream. So from society's point of view, more grips get produced than is actually efficient once that hidden cost is counted.
The framework

Private, External, and Social Cost

To make "market failure" precise, economists split cost (and benefit) into three related ideas.

Private cost — what the producer or consumer themselves actually pays. For Tom, the plastic and electricity behind an $8 grip.

External cost — a cost dumped on someone not involved in the transaction. The pollution cost paid by people near the stream — paid by nobody in the actual sale.

Social cost — the two added together: private cost + external cost. This is the true cost to society of producing that grip.

Whenever social cost is greater than private cost (or social benefit is less than private benefit), that gap is exactly where market failure comes from — the market price only ever reflects the private side.

A different kind of failure

Public Goods vs. Private Goods

Not every good behaves the same way in a market. Two properties decide the difference.

Excludability — can you stop someone who hasn't paid from using it?
Rivalry — does one person using it reduce how much is left for someone else?

A padel court booking is excludable (the club can refuse non-payers) and rivalrous (one match on it means nobody else can use that court then) — a private good.

A streetlight on the path to the club is non-excludable (you can't stop a passer-by benefiting) and non-rivalrous (one walker doesn't leave less light for the next) — a public good.
Why it matters

The Free-Rider Problem

Because a public good is non-excludable, anyone can benefit without paying — free-riding.

If a private firm installed the streetlight, it couldn't charge the passers-by who benefit from it. No profit, no incentive to supply it — even though people genuinely want it. That's why public goods are usually provided by government instead of the market.

Getting the quantity wrong

Merit Goods vs. Demerit Goods

Some goods the market gets the amount wrong on, in a predictable direction.

Merit good — under-provided by the market, because people undervalue the benefit to themselves, or ignore the positive externality to others. E.g. education, healthcare, vaccination.

Demerit good — over-provided by the market, because people undervalue the cost to themselves, or ignore the negative externality to others. E.g. cigarettes, junk food.
