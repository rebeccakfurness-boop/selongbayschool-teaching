Lesson 7 — Comprehensive Teaching Script

Syllabus ref: 2.9 Market failure — definitions, causes & consequences
Linked materials: worksheet.html / worksheet.docx · lesson.html (interactive) / slides.pptx · flashcards.txt

A note before you start

This lesson combines what were originally two separate lessons (definitions, then causes & consequences) into one longer session, so it covers the whole of 2.9 in one sitting. Budget 45–50 minutes rather than the usual 30, and don't feel you need to rush the second half if the first half (externalities, public goods, merit/demerit goods) took a while to land — those four ideas are the foundation everything else in this lesson builds on. If time is genuinely short, the natural pause point is right after Slide 14 (the halfway hook) — the first half stands alone as the definitions, and you can pick up the causes-and-consequences half fresh another day.

This is a full script, not just a lesson outline — written so you can follow it slide-by-slide live (using slides.pptx as backup, or the interactive lesson.html), with what to say, what to ask, what he might ask back, and extra depth if he wants it. Paraphrase in your own voice, but every idea should land before you move on. Lesson 6 ended by flagging that "public goods" and "externalities" would come back properly here — remind him of that if he doesn't recall it himself.

Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson he can:

  1. Define market failure, and explain it using private cost/benefit vs. social cost/benefit.
  2. Define externality, name its four types (negative/positive × production/consumption), and classify real scenarios.
  3. Distinguish private goods from public goods using excludability and rivalry, and explain the free-rider problem.
  4. Distinguish merit goods from demerit goods, and explain why the market under- or over-provides each.
  5. Explain the difference between market output and socially efficient output.
  6. Explain why negative externalities cause overproduction, and positive externalities/merit goods cause underproduction, and why public goods get zero private provision.
  7. Identify which groups (consumers, producers, government) are affected by the consequences of market failure.

Slide-by-slide script

Slide 1 — Title

Say: "Last lesson I promised we'd come back properly to two things I only teased — externalities and public goods. Today's the day: market failure, the topic covering exactly where the price mechanism breaks down — and this is a longer session, since we're covering both what it is and what it actually causes."

Slide 2 — Quick recap

Say: "Quick recap of Lesson 6 first."
Ask him: "In a market economy, what actually decides what gets produced, how, and for whom?"
What a good answer sounds like: What — whatever earns a profit. How — however keeps costs lowest. For whom — whoever is willing and able to pay.

Slide 3 — Warm-up: PED & PES practice

Say: "Before today's new topic, let's keep PED and PES sharp — both keep showing up in exam questions even once we've moved past their own topics."
Work through both together, letting him calculate on paper before you confirm each:

1. "Padel club energy drinks: price $3 to $3.60, that's +20%. Weekly sales fall from 80 to 56, that's −30%. Calculate the PED and classify it." (PED = −30% ÷ 20% = −1.5 → elastic — plenty of substitute drinks nearby.)
2. "Tom's Print Co: a sudden trend pushes the price of his desk organisers up from $15 to $18, that's +20%. He ramps up weekly output from 40 to 44, that's +10%. Calculate the PES and classify it." (PES = 10% ÷ 20% = 0.5 → inelastic — his printer capacity can't scale output as fast as the price is rising.)

Slide 4 — Hook

Say: "Remember the plastic waste from Tom's printer ending up in the stream, and the streetlights nobody privately installs on the path to the padel club? Both of those are market failure in action. This lesson has two halves: first, proper names for exactly what's going wrong in each case — then, once we can name it, exactly what it causes."

Slide 5 — Defining market failure

Say: "Market failure is when the price mechanism fails to allocate resources efficiently — it leads to either too much or too little of something being produced, compared to what's actually best for society as a whole."
Ask him: "In the stream-pollution example, is Tom's printer producing more or less than what's actually best for society, once you account for the pollution?"
What a good answer sounds like: More — the market price only reflects Tom's own costs, not the cost to people near the stream, so from society's point of view too many grips get produced at that price.

Slide 6 — Private, external, and social cost/benefit

Say: "To make that precise, economists split cost and benefit into three related ideas. Private cost is what the producer or consumer themselves pays. External cost is the cost dumped on someone else who wasn't part of the deal. Social cost is simply the two added together — private cost plus external cost. The exact same split applies to benefit."
Ask him: "For a padel grip Tom sells for $8, what's the private cost, and what's the external cost?"
What a good answer sounds like: Private cost — the plastic and electricity Tom actually pays for, reflected in the $8. External cost — the cost of the pollution in the stream, paid by nobody involved in the transaction.
Extra context: Whenever social cost is greater than private cost (or social benefit is less than private benefit), that gap is exactly where market failure comes from — the price only ever reflects the private side.

Slide 7 — Externalities defined

Say: "That external cost or benefit — the bit that spills onto a third party not involved in the transaction — is called an externality. There are four types, sorted by whether they happen at production or consumption, and whether they're negative or positive."
Ask him: "Which of the four do you think the stream pollution is?"
What a good answer sounds like: A negative production externality — it's a cost created while Tom is producing, imposed on someone who isn't part of the sale.

Slide 8 — All four types, with examples

Say: "Four cards, one for each type — tap through them."
Negative production externality: Tom's plastic waste polluting the stream while he manufactures.
Negative consumption externality: Loud music from a late padel match disturbing neighbours who aren't playing or paying.
Positive production externality: A classic textbook one — a beekeeper's bees pollinate a neighbouring farmer's crops as a side effect of producing honey, with nobody paying for that benefit.
Positive consumption externality: Someone getting a flu vaccine also reduces the chance everyone around them catches it — a benefit to people who never got the jab themselves.
If he asks why the beekeeper example matters if it's not Tom-related: Exam questions often use classic textbook contexts like this one directly — worth recognising it by name, not just the Tom's Print Co version.

Slide 9 — Now you try: classify the externality

Read each scenario and ask him to name which of the four types it is, then discuss:
1. "A padel court resurfacing project kicks up dust that settles on a neighbour's washing line." (Negative production externality.)
2. "A homeowner who repaints their house nicely raises the whole street's property values, including neighbours who never asked for it." (Positive production or consumption externality — accept either with reasoning; commonly classed as a production-side spillover from the improvement work.)
3. "Someone smoking a cigarette on the padel club terrace forces nearby players to breathe second-hand smoke." (Negative consumption externality.)

Slide 10 — Public goods vs. private goods

Say: "Second big idea today: not every good behaves the same way in a market. Two properties decide the difference. Excludability — can you stop someone who hasn't paid from using it? Rivalry — does one person using it reduce how much is left for someone else?"
Ask him: "Is a padel court booking excludable and rivalrous?"
What a good answer sounds like: Yes to both — the club can refuse entry to non-payers (excludable), and if Tom's on the court, someone else can't use that same court at that same time (rivalrous). That makes it a private good.
Then ask: "Now what about a streetlight on the path to the club?"
What a good answer sounds like: Not excludable — you can't stop a passer-by benefiting from the light even if they never paid for it. Not rivalrous — one person walking under it doesn't leave less light for the next person. That makes it a public good.

Slide 11 — The free-rider problem

Say: "Because a public good is non-excludable, anyone can benefit without paying — that's called free-riding. Why does that cause market failure?"
What a good answer sounds like: If a private firm installed the streetlight, it couldn't charge the passers-by who benefit from it, so there's no profit in providing it — the market simply won't supply it, even though people genuinely want it. That's why public goods are usually provided by government instead.

Slide 12 — Public or private good?

Guess-then-reveal, one at a time:
1. "A 3D-printed padel grip Tom sells." (Private good — excludable and rivalrous.)
2. "National flood defences protecting an entire coastline." (Public good — classic example: non-excludable, non-rivalrous.)
3. "A padel club membership." (Private good — the club can exclude non-members, and a place on a busy court is rivalrous.)

Slide 13 — Merit goods vs. demerit goods

Say: "Last idea of this half: some goods the market gets the quantity wrong on, in a predictable direction. A merit good is one the market under-provides, because people undervalue the benefit to themselves, or ignore the positive externality to others — think education or healthcare. A demerit good is the opposite: over-provided, because people undervalue the cost to themselves, or ignore the negative externality to others — think cigarettes or junk food."
Ask him: "Playing padel regularly has real health and social benefits players often don't fully weigh up when deciding whether to sign up. Is that closer to a merit good or a demerit good?"
What a good answer sounds like: Merit good — left purely to individual choice, some people under-value the long-term health benefit and under-consume it, so there's a case for encouraging or subsidising it.

Slide 14 — Merit or demerit?

Guess-then-reveal:
1. "Cigarettes sold at a kiosk near the club." (Demerit good — smokers tend to undervalue the long-term health cost to themselves, and impose a negative externality via second-hand smoke.)
2. "A subsidised after-school coding class Tom might run using his 3D printer." (Merit good — parents/students may undervalue the long-term benefit, and society benefits too from more STEM skills.)

This is the natural halfway point — the first half (Slides 1–14) stands alone as "market failure: key definitions" if you need to split the session.

Slide 15 — Halfway hook

Say: "We've now named four kinds of market failure — externalities, public goods, merit goods, demerit goods. But naming a problem isn't the same as knowing its effect. Second half: exactly what goes wrong with the amount of stuff that gets produced, who ends up affected, and a first look at what governments actually do about it."

Slide 16 — Market output vs. socially efficient output

Say: "Market failure has a real, measurable consequence — the market produces the wrong amount of something."
Ask him: "What's the difference between what a market actually produces, and what would genuinely be best for society?"
What a good answer sounds like: Market output only counts private cost and private benefit. Socially efficient output counts the full social cost and social benefit, including third parties. Whenever they don't match, that gap is the consequence of market failure.

Slide 17 — The three consequences

Say: "Now we connect the two halves. Each type of failure we named causes a specific, predictable consequence."
Negative externalities → overproduction. Take Tom's stream pollution — social cost is higher than private cost, but the market only "sees" private cost, so production keeps going past the point it should stop.
Positive externalities & merit goods → underproduction. Take Tom's free coding workshop — social benefit is higher than private benefit, but the market only "sees" private benefit, so less of it gets provided than would actually be best.
Public goods → zero provision. Because of the free-rider problem, a private firm can't capture any profit from providing something like streetlights, so the market provides none at all.
Ask him: "So which of the three applies to a demerit good like cigarettes?"
What a good answer sounds like: Overproduction — a demerit good is essentially a negative externality/cost people underweight, so the market supplies more of it than is socially efficient.

Slide 18 — Now you try: classify the consequence

Read each scenario and ask him to classify it as over-provided, under-provided, or not provided at all, then discuss:
1. "Tom keeps printing extra grips even though the pollution cost is never paid by him." (Over-provided — negative externality.)
2. "A free vaccination programme reaches fewer people than would benefit society." (Under-provided — positive externality/merit good.)
3. "Nobody builds a lighthouse, because no single ship owner will pay for something every ship benefits from for free." (Not provided at all — public good.)
4. "Padel club beginner coaching is offered less often than would be ideal, since the club only counts its own ticket revenue." (Under-provided.)
5. "A factory keeps operating at full output despite uncompensated noise pollution for residents." (Over-provided.)

Slide 19 — Who bears the consequences?

Say: "Three groups feel this differently: consumers, producers, and government."
Ask him: "Why might a producer never fix a negative externality on their own?"
What a good answer sounds like: There's no market signal telling them to change — nobody's actually charging them for the externality they create, so from their own perspective, nothing looks wrong.

Slide 20 — A first look at government's toolkit

Say: "So governments do step in. Quick preview, four tools: taxes to discourage demerit goods and negative externalities, subsidies to encourage merit goods and positive externalities, regulation, and direct provision of public goods. We'll go deep on each of these next lesson — today's just the headline list."

Slide 21 — Quick questions

Run through all four practice cards together.

Slide 22 — Quick check

Run the four-question quiz — two cover the definitions half, two cover the causes-and-consequences half.

Slide 23 — Recap + next lesson

Say: "Market failure happens when the price mechanism misallocates resources. Externalities, public goods, and merit/demerit goods are the causes. Overproduction, underproduction, and zero provision are the consequences — and they affect consumers, producers, and government differently. Next lesson: the actual tools — taxes, subsidies, regulation, price controls — and mixed economies."

Wrap-up
