Lesson 8 — Comprehensive Teaching Script

Syllabus ref: 2.9 Market failure — causes & consequences
Linked materials: worksheet.html / worksheet.docx · lesson.html (interactive) / slides.pptx · flashcards.txt

This is a full script, not just an outline — written so you can follow it step-by-step live using the interactive lesson.html. Lesson 7 named the four types of market failure; today's job is to explain exactly what each one does — the measurable consequence for how much gets produced — and set up next lesson's topic (government intervention) without going into its detail yet.

Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson he can:

  1. Explain the difference between market output and socially efficient output.
  2. Explain why negative externalities cause overproduction, and positive externalities/merit goods cause underproduction.
  3. Explain why public goods are not provided at all by a pure free market.
  4. Identify which groups (consumers, producers, government) are affected by the consequences of market failure.

Slide-by-slide script

Slide 1 — Title

Say: "Last lesson we named four kinds of market failure. Today: what each one actually does — exactly what goes wrong with the amount of stuff that gets produced."

Slide 2 — Quick recap

Say: "Quick recap first."
Work through all four recap cards together, letting him answer before flipping each one.

Slide 3 — Warm-up: PED & PES practice

Work through both together, letting him calculate on paper before you confirm each:

1. "Tom's Print Co phone cases: price $6 to $7.50, that's +25%. Weekly sales fall from 80 to 56, that's −30%. Calculate the PED and classify it." (PED = −30% ÷ 25% = −1.2 → elastic.)
2. "Padel club racket restringing: price $12 to $15, that's +25%. Weekly slots offered rise from 20 to 22, that's +10%. Calculate the PES and classify it." (PES = 10% ÷ 25% = 0.4 → inelastic — limited staff time to restring more rackets quickly.)

Slide 4 — Hook

Say: "Naming a problem isn't the same as knowing its effect. Today: exactly what goes wrong with the amount produced, who gets affected, and a first look at what governments actually do about it."

Slide 5 — Market output vs. socially efficient output

Say: "Market failure has a real, measurable consequence — the market produces the wrong amount of something."
Ask him: "What's the difference between what a market actually produces, and what would genuinely be best for society?"
What a good answer sounds like: Market output only counts private cost and private benefit. Socially efficient output counts the full social cost and social benefit, including third parties. Whenever they don't match, that gap is the consequence of market failure.

Slide 6 — Negative externalities → overproduction

Say: "Take Tom's stream pollution. Social cost is higher than private cost, but the market only 'sees' private cost."
Ask him: "So does the market produce more or less padel grips than is actually best for society?"
What a good answer sounds like: More — production keeps going past the point where it should stop, because the pollution cost never shows up in Tom's decision-making.

Slide 7 — Positive externalities & merit goods → underproduction

Say: "Now the opposite case — Tom's free coding workshop. Social benefit is higher than private benefit, but the market only 'sees' private benefit."
Ask him: "More or less gets provided than is best for society?"
What a good answer sounds like: Less — Tom (or anyone) has no way to capture the full social value of the workshop, so it gets under-provided relative to what's actually best.

Slide 8 — Public goods → zero provision

Say: "Public goods take this to the extreme."
Ask him: "Given the free-rider problem, how much of a pure public good does a private market actually provide?"
What a good answer sounds like: Little to none — a private firm can't capture profit from a non-excludable good, so there's no incentive to supply it at all, even though people clearly want it.

Slide 9 — Now you try: classify the consequence

Read each scenario and ask him to classify it as over-provided, under-provided, or not provided at all, then discuss:
1. "Tom keeps printing extra grips even though the pollution cost is never paid by him." (Over-provided — negative externality.)
2. "A free vaccination programme reaches fewer people than would benefit society." (Under-provided — positive externality/merit good.)
3. "Nobody builds a lighthouse, because no single ship owner will pay for something every ship benefits from for free." (Not provided at all — public good.)
4. "Padel club beginner coaching is offered less often than would be ideal, since the club only counts its own ticket revenue." (Under-provided.)
5. "A factory keeps operating at full output despite uncompensated noise pollution for residents." (Over-provided.)

Slide 10 — Who bears the consequences?

Say: "Three groups feel this differently: consumers, producers, and government."
Ask him: "Why might a producer never fix a negative externality on their own?"
What a good answer sounds like: There's no market signal telling them to change — nobody's actually charging them for the externality they create, so from their own perspective, nothing looks wrong.

Slide 11 — A first look at government's toolkit

Say: "So governments do step in. Quick preview, four tools: taxes to discourage demerit goods and negative externalities, subsidies to encourage merit goods and positive externalities, regulation, and direct provision of public goods. We'll go deep on each of these next lesson — today's just the headline list."

Slide 12 — Quick questions

Run through all four practice cards together.

Slide 13 — Quick check

Run the three-question quiz.

Slide 14 — Recap

Say: "Negative externalities cause overproduction. Positive externalities and merit goods cause underproduction. Public goods get zero private provision. All three affect consumers, producers, and government differently — and next lesson we see exactly how government responds."

Slide 15 — Next lesson

Say: "Next time: the actual tools — taxes, subsidies, regulation, price controls — and mixed economies."

Wrap-up

Set the worksheet for self-directed time, and mention flashcards.txt for NotebookLM. The market-output-vs-socially-efficient-output framework introduced today is the foundation for every government intervention lesson from here on — if that core idea (the gap between what's produced and what's best for society) isn't solid by the end, it's worth a very quick verbal recap before moving on, since everything in Lesson 9 builds directly on it.
