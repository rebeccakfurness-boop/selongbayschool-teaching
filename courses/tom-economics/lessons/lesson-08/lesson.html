Hook

So What Actually Happens?

Last lesson we named four kinds of market failure — externalities, public goods, merit goods, demerit goods. But naming a problem isn't the same as knowing its effect.

Today: exactly what goes wrong with the amount of stuff that gets produced, who ends up affected by it, and a first look at what governments actually do about it.
New idea

Market Output vs. Socially Efficient Output

Market failure isn't just a label. It has a real, measurable consequence: the market produces the wrong amount of something.

Market output — how much actually gets produced, based on private cost and private benefit alone.

Socially efficient output — how much should get produced, once you count social cost and social benefit (the true cost/benefit to everyone, not just the people in the transaction).

Whenever these two numbers don't match, that gap is the consequence of market failure.
Consequence 1

Negative Externalities → Overproduction

Take Tom's stream pollution. Social cost is higher than private cost — but the market only "sees" private cost, so production keeps going past the point where it actually should stop.

Result: more grips get produced and sold than is actually best for society, once the pollution is counted.
Consequence 2

Positive Externalities & Merit Goods → Underproduction

The opposite happens with positive externalities and merit goods, like Tom's free coding workshop. Social benefit is higher than private benefit — but the market only "sees" private benefit.

Result: less of it gets provided than would actually be best for society.
Consequence 3

Public Goods → Zero Provision

Public goods take this to the extreme. Because of the free-rider problem, a private firm can't capture any profit from providing something like streetlights.

Result: the market provides none at all, even though people clearly want it.

New idea

Who Bears the Consequences?

Consumers — may pay for goods that quietly harm others, or miss out on merit goods that would genuinely benefit them.

Producers — get no market signal telling them to change, since nobody's actually charging them for the externality they create.

Government — has to decide whether the gap between market and socially efficient output is big enough to justify stepping in, and how.

A First Look at Government's Toolkit

So governments do step in. A quick preview before we go deep on each one next lesson.

Taxes — to discourage demerit goods and negative externalities

Subsidies — to encourage merit goods and positive externalities

Regulation — rules and limits on behaviour

Direct provision — government builds or provides public goods itself
