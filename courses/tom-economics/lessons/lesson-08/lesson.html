Hook

Time for the Toolkit

Last lesson ended with a quick preview: taxes, subsidies, regulation, direct provision — the tools government uses when a market fails.

Today we go deep on two of them: taxes & subsidies, and a new idea, price controls. All three change what a market produces, and all three show up constantly in exam questions.
New idea

What Is a Mixed Economic System?

Lesson 6 covered the market economic system: the price mechanism alone decides what, how, and for whom. But almost no real country works that way.

A mixed economic system combines the market (private sector) with government intervention (public sector). The market still does most of the day-to-day allocating — but government steps in wherever the market fails, to correct or soften the problem.

Most real-world economies — including the one Tom's Print Co operates in — are mixed, not purely market or purely planned.
Why intervene?

Government's Job: Fix What the Market Gets Wrong

Remember the causes of market failure from last lesson? Government intervention exists to address exactly those.

Negative externalities (overproduction) → discourage them.
Positive externalities / merit goods (underproduction) → encourage them.
Public goods (zero provision) → provide them directly.
Unfair prices for buyers or sellers → control them directly.

Today's two tools — tax/subsidy and price controls — tackle the first three problems in different ways.
Tool 1

Indirect Taxes

An indirect tax is a tax on spending, charged to the seller but usually passed on to the buyer through a higher price.

Effect: it raises the producer's cost, so supply shifts left — price goes up, quantity goes down.

Example: the government adds a tax to padel club energy drinks (a demerit good). Tom's Print Co isn't affected directly, but the club's drinks now cost more, and it sells fewer of them — exactly the overproduction problem from last lesson, corrected.

Ask your tutor to sketch the shift with you: supply curve moves left/up, new equilibrium has a higher price and lower quantity.

Tool 2

Subsidies

A subsidy is a payment from government to producers that lowers their cost of production.

Effect: it lowers the producer's cost, so supply shifts right — price goes down, quantity goes up.

Example: the local council offers Tom a subsidy to run more of his free coding workshops (a merit good). His effective cost of running each one drops, so he can afford to offer more of them — exactly the underproduction problem from last lesson, corrected.

A different kind of tool

Price Controls: Two Types

Tax and subsidy work by changing supply. Price controls work differently — government sets a legal limit on price itself, and the market isn't allowed to move past it.

Maximum price (a "price ceiling") — a legal limit the price can't go above.

Minimum price (a "price floor") — a legal limit the price can't go below.
Price control 1

Maximum Price → Shortage

A maximum price is set below the free-market equilibrium price, to make something more affordable.

Example: the local council caps padel court rental at $10/hour, well below what the market would naturally charge at peak times.

Effect: at that low price, quantity demanded is higher than quantity supplied — a shortage. Courts run out fast, and people who'd happily pay more miss out entirely.

Ask your tutor to sketch this: a horizontal price line below equilibrium, with quantity demanded stretching further right than quantity supplied.

Price control 2

Minimum Price → Surplus

A minimum price is set above the free-market equilibrium price, usually to protect sellers' income.

Example: the government sets a minimum wage above what some employers would otherwise pay.

Effect: at that high price, quantity supplied is higher than quantity demanded — a surplus. For a minimum wage specifically, that surplus shows up as unemployment: more people want jobs at that wage than there are jobs available.
