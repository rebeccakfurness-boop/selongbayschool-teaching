The big idea

Four Categories, Every Business, Always

Every single thing any business produces — a Lego set, a haircut, a padel court booking, a 3D-printed grip — is only possible because it combines resources from four categories. Economists call these the factors of production.

No matter how different two businesses are, everything they use to produce something sorts into one of just four boxes: Land, Labour, Capital, or Enterprise. Spotting which one something is — that's the actual exam skill. Let's go through each one properly, with Tom's Print Co. as our example the whole way through.
Factor 1 of 4

🌍 Land

Definition: Land means any natural resource used in production — not just literal ground or soil. It includes raw materials, minerals, oil, water, forests — anything nature provides that a business uses.
In Tom's Print Co.: the PLA plastic filament Tom prints with is made from petroleum — a natural resource. Even though a spool of filament doesn't look anything like "land" in the everyday sense, in economics it counts as land, because it's a raw material taken from nature and turned into something usable.
💰 Reward for supplying land: RENT
Factor 2 of 4

🧑‍🔧 Labour

Definition: Labour means human physical or mental effort used in production — anyone actually doing the work.
In Tom's Print Co.: every hour Tom spends designing a CAD file, running the printer, sanding a rough edge, or packaging an order for posting — that's labour. If Tom hired a friend to help pack orders, their time would be labour too.
💰 Reward for supplying labour: WAGES
Factor 3 of 4

🖨️ Capital

Definition: Capital means manufactured goods used to produce other goods or services — machinery, tools, equipment, buildings.
In Tom's Print Co.: the 3D printer itself, Tom's laptop for designing, his calipers and sandpaper — all capital.
⚠️ The #1 mix-up: in everyday language "capital" often means money or savings. In economics it does not. Capital is the physical machinery itself. The cash Tom used to buy the printer is finance, not capital — the printer sitting in his workshop is the capital.
💰 Reward for supplying capital: INTEREST
Factor 4 of 4

🎲 Enterprise

Definition: Enterprise means the willingness and ability to take risks and organise the other three factors, in pursuit of profit.
In Tom's Print Co.: Tom deciding to actually start the business — choosing what to make, risking his own savings on a printer that might not pay off, organising the land, labour and capital into something that works. That decision-making and risk-taking is enterprise.
💰 Reward for supplying enterprise: PROFIT

Profit is different from the other three rewards. Rent, wages and interest are usually agreed in advance and guaranteed. Profit is what's left over — and it can be negative.

A second dimension

Factors Can Also Change

So far we've named the four factors. But each one can also change in two different ways:

QUANTITY means how much of a factor exists — more machines, more workers, more raw material available. It's more of the same.
QUALITY means how good that factor is — a better machine, a more skilled worker, a stronger material. It's the same amount, but improved.

Example: Tom buying a second 3D printer is a change in quantity of capital. Tom upgrading to a faster, more precise printer is a change in quality of capital — same number of machines, just a better one.
