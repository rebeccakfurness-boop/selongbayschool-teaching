Lesson 1 — Teaching Guide & Script

Syllabus ref: 1.2 Factors of production (1.2.1 Factors of production and their rewards · 1.2.2 Quantity and quality of factors of production)
Linked materials: worksheet.md · slides.html · flashcards.txt — all four cover this same syllabus point.

Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson Tom can:

  1. Define the four factors of production — land, labour, capital, enterprise — and give an example of each.
  2. State the reward each factor earns — rent, wages, interest, profit.
  3. Explain what causes the quantity and quality of a factor of production to change.

The running example: Tom's Print Co.

Use this the whole lesson — a small 3D-printing business Tom "runs," making custom padel paddle overgrips and personalised Lego-compatible minifigure accessories to sell to friends at school and the padel club. Every definition below hooks onto it. (This business reappears in later lessons on firms, costs and production — worth bedding in properly now.)

Timing breakdown (30 minutes)

0–3 min — Hook
Ask Tom: "If you were going to actually start Tom's Print Co. this weekend, what four types of thing would you need — not a shopping list, but categories?" Let him guess loosely. Don't correct yet — just plant the question.

3–15 min — Core teaching: the four factors + their rewards
Introduce each one anchored to the business:

Factor Definition In Tom's Print Co. Reward
Land Natural resources used in production — not just "ground," anything nature provides The PLA filament spool is made from petroleum, a natural resource; the workshop space Rent
Labour Human physical or mental effort used in production Tom's own hours designing CAD files, running the printer, sanding, packaging Wages
Capital Manufactured goods used to produce other goods/services (not money) The 3D printer, the laptop, calipers, sandpaper Interest
Enterprise The willingness and ability to take risks, organise the other three factors, and innovate, in pursuit of profit Tom's decision to start the business at all, choose what to make, risk his savings on a printer that might not pay off Profit

Say this out loud explicitly: "Profit is different from the other three rewards — rent, wages and interest are usually agreed in advance and guaranteed. Profit is what's left over, and it can be negative." This is the single most exam-useful line in the lesson.

15–23 min — Quantity vs quality
Explain that factors of production don't stay fixed — both how much of a factor exists and how good it is can change:

  • Quantity of capital ↑ — Tom buys a second 3D printer.
  • Quality of capital ↑ — Tom upgrades to a faster, more precise printer (same number of machines, better ones).
  • Quantity of labour ↑ — a friend joins to help pack and sell.
  • Quality of labour ↑ — Tom does a short online CAD course and gets much faster/better at designing.
  • Quantity/quality of land — finding a new filament supplier (quantity) vs switching to a higher-grade filament that prints stronger parts (quality).

Ask Tom to generate one more pair himself before moving on (padel club buying more courts vs resurfacing existing courts is a good nudge if he's stuck).

23–28 min — Guided practice
Run through worksheet Section B verbally together (don't just hand it over) — point at each item in the Tom's Print Co. scenario and ask "which factor, and why?" before he writes anything.

28–30 min — Wrap-up
State the plan for self-directed time: finish the worksheet, paste flashcards.txt into NotebookLM. Preview next lesson: "Next time we look at what happens when there's suddenly way more demand for padel court bookings than there are courts available — shortages, and how prices react."

Common misconceptions to watch for

  1. "Capital = money." In economics, capital is the physical manufactured stuff (the printer, tools) — not cash or savings. Cash is finance, not a factor of production. Correct this immediately if it comes up.
  2. Mixing up labour and enterprise. An employee Tom hires supplies labour (wages); Tom himself, organising and risking capital, supplies enterprise (profit).
  3. "Land only means soil/ground." In this syllabus land = any natural resource — oil, minerals, fish stocks, the raw filament material itself.
  4. Treating profit as guaranteed like wages. It's a residual reward and the only one that can be negative (a loss).
