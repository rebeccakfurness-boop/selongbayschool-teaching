Lesson 1 — Comprehensive Teaching Script

Cambridge Primary Science, Stage 6 · Unit 1.1 The circulatory system
Linked materials: worksheet.html / worksheet.docx · lesson.html (interactive)

Rebuild note: this replaces the bare worksheet Mila had last time, which asked her to independently research a full vein/artery/capillary comparison table with no teaching first — reasonably too hard for a first-ever science lesson. This version teaches everything needed before asking her to apply it, and restructures the order: organs in the body → what the heart does → what blood carries → then blood vessels, gently. It's a full 40-minute script with a question built into nearly every step — don't let any step pass without her actually answering something, even just out loud.

Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson she can:

  1. Name several organs and roughly where they're found in the body.
  2. Explain that the heart is a muscle whose job is to pump blood, and that this is felt as a heartbeat/pulse.
  3. Explain that blood carries food and oxygen to cells, and carries waste away.
  4. Name the three parts of the circulatory system, and the three types of blood vessel with one distinguishing fact about each.
  5. Describe, in order, the loop blood travels: heart → lungs → heart → body → heart.

Step-by-step script

Step 1 — What's inside you?

Say: "Today's your first proper science lesson, so we're going right back to basics — the parts inside your own body."
Ask her: "Have a guess — which organ do you think pumps blood around your body? And can you name one organ in your head?"
Let her guess freely, don't correct yet — we check both guesses very soon.

Step 2 — Meet some organs

Six tap-cards: brain, heart, lungs, stomach, kidneys, intestines.
For each one, before she taps: "What do you think this one does?" Only after her guess, let her tap to check.

Step 3 — Where is it? (sort activity)

She sorts each organ into Head / Chest / Tummy. Let her do this herself without help first — if she gets one wrong, ask "why do you think it might actually be there?" rather than just telling her.

Step 4 — Meet your heart

Say: "Put your hand flat on your chest right now."
Ask her: "Point to exactly where you think your heart is."
Then reveal the diagram together and compare to her guess. Ask: "Was your guess close? Now make a fist — that's about the size of your own heart!"

Step 5 — Why does your heart beat?

Say: "Your heart is a muscle. Its one job, all day, all night, without ever stopping, is to pump blood round your whole body."
Ask her: "What do you think would happen to the rest of your body if your heart stopped, even for a few seconds?" Let her reason it through — she doesn't need a perfect answer, just the instinct that everything depends on that pumping.

Step 6 — Feel your own pulse

Do this together, physically. Say: "Two fingers on your wrist, or the side of your neck. Stay still. Can you feel a little beat?"
Once she's found it: "Let's count together — how many beats in 15 seconds?" Multiply by 4 together. Ask: "Is that faster or slower than you expected?"

Step 7 — What does blood actually carry?

Ask her first: "Why do you think your cells might need things delivered to them, like a delivery service?" Then reveal: food and oxygen going out, waste (carbon dioxide) coming back.

Step 8 — The 3 parts of the circulatory system

Ask her: "Can you name all three parts from memory, before you look?" (Heart, blood, blood vessels.) This is a genuine recall check, not a guess — she's just met all three individually across the last few steps.

Step 9 — Meet the blood vessels

Say: "Blood travels through tubes, a bit like water through pipes."
Ask her a real prediction question: "Right after a heartbeat, blood gets pushed out hard. Which tube do you think needs thicker, stronger walls — the one right next to that push, or the one on the calm journey back?" Let her reason about pressure before the next step confirms it.

Step 10 — Arteries, veins & capillaries (tap cards)

Check her prediction from Step 9 against what the cards actually say. If she predicted correctly, tell her so explicitly — that's a real scientific prediction she got right.

Step 11 — Artery, vein, or capillary? (sort activity)

Five statements to classify. The last one is deliberately tricky (an artery to the lungs, even though it carries no-oxygen blood) — if she gets this one wrong, that's completely expected on a first pass. Explain: "Arteries and veins are named by which way the blood is travelling, not by what's in it." This is the exact idea her original worksheet expected her to work out alone — now she's meeting it with full support.

Step 12 — The journey of blood

Read all six steps of the loop together, out loud. Then ask her to recite it back from memory, no peeking: heart → lungs → heart → body → heart.

Step 13 — The loop diagram

Ask her: "Point to the exact spot on the diagram where blood picks up oxygen. Now point to where it drops that oxygen off." (Lungs; her own body's cells.)

Step 14 — Quick questions

Seven guess-then-reveal cards, covering everything so far. Work through all of them — this is real consolidation, not filler.

Step 15 — Quick check (quiz)

Five multiple-choice questions. If she gets one wrong, don't just show the right answer — ask her to explain her reasoning first, then correct gently.

Step 16 — Recap + homework

Say: "Today: organs are the parts inside you that each do a job. Your heart is a muscle that pumps blood, non-stop. Blood carries food and oxygen out, and waste back. Arteries go away from the heart, veins go back, capillaries connect them. And it all moves in a loop: heart, lungs, heart, body, heart, forever."
Set the worksheet for her own time.

Wrap-up

This lesson is deliberately full of small physical/verbal check-ins rather than long stretches of reading — for a first-ever science lesson, the goal is that she's never passive for more than about a minute at a time. If any single idea feels shaky by the end (the pressure/wall-thickness reasoning and the "artery to the lungs" exception are the two most likely candidates), it's worth a very quick extra verbal round-trip on just that one idea before moving to the worksheet.
